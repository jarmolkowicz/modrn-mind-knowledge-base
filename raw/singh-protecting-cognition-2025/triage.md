## Summary

A short workshop position paper (CHI '25 "Tools for Thought") that **synthesizes** the existing literature on GenAI's effects on human cognition and organizes it through two established educational-psychology frameworks. It is explicitly novice-focused (students) and explicitly one-sided by design: "while GenAI can both augment and hinder cognitive development … this work primarily examines the latter and explores strategies to protect essential cognitive abilities."

Section 2 catalogs emerging cognitive challenges (knowledge acquisition → passive consumption/echo chambers; reasoning → over-reliance on AI advice; learning → short-term performance without retention/transfer; plus creativity, metacognition, critical thinking). Section 3 supplies the paper's distinctive contribution — two **framing lenses**: (a) **Krathwohl's revised Bloom's Taxonomy**, used to argue GenAI lets learners jump straight to synthesized factual/procedural knowledge while *bypassing* the middle cognitive-process rungs (remember → understand → apply → analyze → evaluate), which are where metacognitive skill is built; and (b) **Dewey's theory of reflective thinking**, used to show how GenAI disrupts the *prerequisites* of reflective thought (a state of doubt; prior knowledge; active persistent consideration; suspended judgment/tolerance for uncertainty; connecting & evaluating ideas). Section 4 turns Dewey's prerequisites into a **design + diagnostic framework**: use unmet prerequisites to (i) *identify passive AI use* and (ii) *design interventions* (highlighting, injected friction, schema/knowledge-graph tools, metacognitive prompts, AI "provocations," staged guardrails that minimize AI early in learning).

The paper is a secondary source: nearly every phenomenon it names is already in the KB from primary/stronger sources. Its value is the organizing lenses and the education-design synthesis, not new evidence.
