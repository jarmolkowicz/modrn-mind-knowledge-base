## Summary

A **preregistered field experiment** with **791 experienced professionals at Procter & Gamble**, working on **real product-innovation challenges** from their own business units over one working day, with real professional stakes (best proposals presented to business-unit leaders; some entered P&G's actual innovation pipeline). A 2×2 design crossed *team structure* (individual vs. cross-functional dyad of one commercial + one R&D professional) with *AI access* (with vs. without GenAI). Outcomes were scored by multiple independent evaluators. This is top-tier causal evidence — peer-reviewed in *Organization Science*, published online 12 Jun 2026.

The paper reframes GenAI from **tool** to **"cybernetic teammate"** and tests whether it can fill the three classic pillars of teamwork: **performance**, **expertise integration**, and **social/emotional engagement**. Headline results: (1) **Performance** — individuals with AI (+0.37 SD over solo baseline) *matched two-person teams without AI* (+0.24 SD); Team+AI (+0.39 SD) was not significantly better than solo+AI, i.e. AI's gain comes mostly from boosting individual cognition rather than improving human-to-human collaboration. (2) **Expertise / silo-bridging** — without AI, R&D professionals proposed technical solutions and commercial professionals proposed commercial ones; *with AI, both produced balanced, boundary-spanning solutions on their own*, replicating what cross-functional teaming achieves. (3) **Social/emotional** — AI's conversational interface produced *more positive* affect (excitement, enthusiasm; less anxiety/frustration), partly filling teamwork's motivational role.

Two further findings matter for the KB: (a) a **generation-vs-evaluation asymmetry** — AI raises the average quality of *generated* ideas (shifts the whole distribution up, does **not** homogenize; Team+AI ~3× as likely to produce top-decile breakthroughs), while **human judgment retains value in evaluative selection**; and (b) **diminishing marginal returns** — the first teammate (human *or* AI) delivers most of the average gain, but the *second* addition (Team+AI) disproportionately lifts the right tail (breakthroughs). Authors flag open risks: long-term skill/expertise development, trust, and a possible self-reinforcing preference for AI over human teammates.
